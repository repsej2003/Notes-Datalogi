## Question 1

**1.1 — True.** M3 with $c:=a$ reads $\mathrm{dist}(a,a)\le\mathrm{dist}(a,b)+\mathrm{dist}(b,a)$; by M1 the left side is $0$ and by M2 the right side is $2\,\mathrm{dist}(a,b)$, hence $\mathrm{dist}(a,b)\ge0$.

**1.2 — False.** M1 holds (squaring is injective on $[0,\infty)$) and M2 is clear, but M3 fails: $\mathrm{dist}(0,2)=4>\mathrm{dist}(0,1)+\mathrm{dist}(1,2)=2$. Worth internalizing: the squared Euclidean distance is exactly what the squared loss averages, and it is *not* a distance — a loss function need not be a metric.

**1.3 — True.** Values lie in $\{0,1\}$, so M1 and M2 are immediate. For M3: if $a=c$ the left side is $0\le$ right side; if $a\ne c$ the left side is $1$, and the right side is at least $1$ because $a=b$ would force $b\ne c$ (as $a\ne c$), while $a\ne b$ already contributes $1$.

**1.4 — False.** Take $\mathcal{X}=\{p,q,r\}$ with $\mathrm{dist}(p,q)=\mathrm{dist}(q,r)=1$, $\mathrm{dist}(p,r)=3$, and $0$ on the diagonal. M1 holds (zeros exactly on the diagonal), M2 and non-negativity hold, but $\mathrm{dist}(p,r)=3>1+1$. The axioms are independent — none is implied by the others (the constant-zero function violates only M1; 1.2 violates only M3).

**1.5 — True.** M1, M2, and non-negativity are inherited from the injectivity, symmetry, and non-negativity of $t\mapsto|t|^p$. M3 fails on $a=(1,0)$, $b=(0,1)$ via the origin: $\mathrm{dist}_p(a,b)=2^{1/p}>2=1+1=\mathrm{dist}_p(a,0)+\mathrm{dist}_p(0,b)$, since $\frac1p>1$. This is the notes' subadditivity failure: for $0<p<1$ the expression is not a norm, and the pair it defines is not a metric space.

## Question 2

**2.1 — True.** Each term $(w_0+w_1x_i-y_i)^2$ is the square of an affine function of $w$, hence convex, and a sum of convex functions is convex. For a differentiable convex $F$ every stationary point is a global minimizer: expanding $F(v)-F(u)-\nabla F(u)^\top(v-u)$ for a convex quadratic cancels the linear terms and leaves a nonnegative remainder, so $F(v)\ge F(u)$ whenever $\nabla F(u)=0$. This is what licenses the closed-form derivation — and, later, gradient descent on this objective: no bad stationary points exist to get stuck in.

**2.2 — False.** Convexity of the *loss* is what makes every stationary point a global minimizer, and not every loss has it. Take $\ell(y,y')=((y-y')^2-1)^2$ — nonnegative and differentiable, but nonconvex in $y'$ — and the single training point $(x_1,y_1)=(0,0)$. Writing $c:=w_0$ for the prediction (the slope $w_1$ multiplies $x_1=0$, so it does not affect the fit), the training error is $f(c)=(c^2-1)^2$, with derivative $f'(c)=4c(c^2-1)$, which vanishes at $c\in\{-1,0,1\}$. At $c=0$ the error is $1$ — in fact a local *maximum* — while at $c=\pm1$ it is $0$, the global minima: a stationary point that is not a global minimizer.

**2.3 — True.** $f\in\mathcal{H}$ and noiselessness give $\widehat R_S(f)=0$; a sum of squares cannot be negative, so the minimum is exactly $0$, every minimizer attains it, and $f$ attains it.

**2.4 — False.** Take $f(x)=1$ (i.e. $w^*=(1,0)$) and the single training point $(0,1)$. Every hypothesis with $h(0)=1$ has zero training error, so all of them are least-squares solutions — for instance $x\mapsto1+x$, which disagrees with $f$ at every $x\ne0$. Zero training error under realizability does not *identify* $f$: what the sample never queries is unconstrained, and which interpolant a learner returns is decided by its inductive bias, not by the data. This is the seed of both overfitting and Chapter 6's negative results.

**2.5 — False.** The empirical risk is an *average* — nothing forces it to be optimal term by term. On $S=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,1)\}$ the least-squares line is $x\mapsto\frac{x}2-\frac16$, erring by $\frac16,\frac13,\frac16$ at the three points; the hypothesis $x\mapsto\frac{x}2$ achieves loss $0$ at the first point, where the fit errs $(\frac1{36})$. Minimizing the average is the definition of ERM — and precisely what leaves room to trade one training point against another.

## Question 3

**3.1 — True.** This is the chapter's Lagrangian derivation, restated: the constrained problem is ERM over the norm-bounded subclass, and the equivalence for each $\eta$ is given. The scheme — one objective pursuing fit and complexity — is structural risk minimization with the subclass index replaced by the norm budget; as $\lambda$ grows, the effective budget shrinks.

**3.2 — True.** Exchange argument, no matrices needed. Let $w_i:=w_{\lambda_i}$ with $\lambda_1<\lambda_2$, write $R_i:=\widehat R_S(w_i)$ and $N_i:=\|w_i\|_2^2$. Optimality of each $w_i$ for *its own* objective gives $R_1+\lambda_1N_1\le R_2+\lambda_1N_2$ and $R_2+\lambda_2N_2\le R_1+\lambda_2N_1$; adding yields $(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)(N_2-N_1)\le0$, so $N_2\le N_1$ — the weights shrink. Substituting $N_2\le N_1$ back into the first inequality gives $R_1\le R_2$. Paying for regularization in training error is not a defect; it is the design.

**3.3 — False.** Let $f\equiv0$, training points $(0,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ (noise realizations $1$ and $-1$), test point $(2,0)$. As $\lambda\to0^+$, the penalty vanishes and the fit approaches the least-squares solution, which here is the unique affine function through both training points: $h(x)=w_0+w_1x$ with $h(0)=1$ giving $w_0=1$ and $h(1)=-1$ giving $w_1=-2$, i.e. the interpolant $x\mapsto1-2x$ with training error $0$. It predicts $1-2\cdot2=-3$ at the test input: test error $(0-(-3))^2=9$. As $\lambda\to\infty$, the penalty dominates the objective, so the minimizer is forced towards the smallest-norm weights, $w_\lambda\to0$ — the predictor $0=f$ with test error $0$. Increasing $\lambda$ improves the test error from $9$ to $0$ — on this data, regularization is all gain. The two monotonicities together are the chapter's U-curve in one example: the training error climbs ($0\to1$) while the test error falls, because the unregularized fit was chasing noise.

**3.4 — True.** Unregularized: the unit change $x'=cx$ induces a bijection $w\mapsto(w_0,w_1/c)$ between parameters that preserves every prediction on every input — the same functions are representable and every training error is unchanged, so the minimum is untouched. Ridge: the penalty is computed from the *parameters*, and the same function now pays a different price — a slope $a$ costs $a^2$ in one recording and $a^2/c^2$ in the other — so the risk/penalty trade-off shifts and the minimizer moves. Example ($m=1$, training point $(x_1,y_1)=(1,1)$, $\lambda=1$, objective $(w_0+w_1x_1-y_1)^2+w_0^2+w_1^2$): with the feature recorded as is, a perfect fit needs $w_0+w_1=1$, and among all $(w_0,w_1)$ on that line the penalty $w_0^2+w_1^2$ is smallest at the point closest to the origin, $(\frac12,\frac12)$ — the closest point on the line $w_0+w_1=1$ is where the line meets the perpendicular direction $(1,1)$, which by symmetry is $(\frac12,\frac12)$, at cost $\frac12$. Balancing fit against penalty, setting the two partial derivatives of $(w_0+w_1-1)^2+w_0^2+w_1^2$ to zero gives $w_\lambda=(\frac13,\frac13)$, predicting $w_0+w_1=\frac23$ at $x_1$. With the feature recorded doubled ($x_1=2$ now, same data), a perfect fit needs $w_0+2w_1=1$; the closest point to the origin on that line is where it meets the perpendicular direction $(1,2)$, namely $(\frac15,\frac25)$, at cost $\frac15$ — the same fit got cheaper — and solving the two derivative equations gives $w_\lambda=(\frac16,\frac13)$, predicting $\frac16+\frac13\cdot2=\frac56\ne\frac23$. Same data, different units, different predictions on the training set. This is why the chapter standardizes features before applying ridge: for regularized methods, the parametrization is part of the model.

**3.5 — False.** Two training points with the *same* feature value but contradictory labels: $(x_1,y_1)=(0,1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)=(0,-1)$ (the sensor reads $0$ on both, and the labels are arbitrary noise). Writing the hypothesis as $h_w(x)=w_0+w_1x$, the prediction at both points is $w_0$ (the feature value is $0$), so the fitting term $\frac12[(w_0-1)^2+(w_0+1)^2]=w_0^2+1$ depends on $w_0$ alone and is minimized at $w_0=0$; the penalty $\lambda(w_0^2+w_1^2)$ is minimized over $w_1$ at $w_1=0$, and $w_1$ appears nowhere else. So $w_\lambda=(0,0)$ exactly, for every $\lambda>0$. The chapter's remark that ridge "shrinks but does not sparsify" is a statement about *generic* data: exact zeros are not a structural property of the $L_2$ penalty the way they are of the $L_1$ corners in LASSO — but the universal claim is refuted by degenerate data (no signal at all, and ridge gives up entirely).

## Question 4

**4.1 — True.** Suppose $h,h'\in W$ disagreed at some training input $x_i$. Their average $\bar h=\frac{h+h'}2$ is again in $\mathcal{H}$, and expanding both sides of $\big(\tfrac{a+b}2-c\big)^2\le\tfrac12\big[(a-c)^2+(b-c)^2\big]$ — strict when $a\ne b$ — shows $\bar h$ matches or improves on both at every training point, with a strict improvement at $x_i$. Summing: $\widehat R_S(\bar h)<\min$ — contradiction. (This averaging mechanism — a convex class, a squared loss, two tied predictors — is exactly what ensemble methods exploit in Chapter 12. Note that agreement of *predictions* does not force agreement of *weights*.)

**4.2 — False.** A single training point with sensor reading $0$ and label $5$, i.e. $(x_1,y_1)=(0,5)$: the hypothesis predicts $h_w(x_1)=w_0+w_1\cdot0=w_0$, so zero training error is exactly the condition $w_0=5$, and $W=\{w:w_0=5\}$ — one minimizer per value of the sensor weight $w_1$, which ranges over all of $\mathbb{R}$. The training error cannot see the sensor at all: it never varied, so the data carries no evidence about it, and the weight is decided entirely by whatever else the learner does. Switching the feature off ($w_1=0$) is one option among infinitely many, not a consequence of ERM.

**4.3 — True.** On the training set the sensor contributes nothing (every training input has sensor reading $0$), so all members of $W$ predict $w_0=5$ there — this is the agreement of tied minimizers on the training inputs. On a fresh input with sensor reading $1$, they predict $5+w_1$, which differs across $W$. Non-uniqueness is invisible on the training set and visible only off it: the hypotheses are genuinely different learning outcomes, tied only on what happened to be observed.

**4.4 — False.** Ridge does not select among the least-squares solutions — it optimizes a *different* objective, willingly trading training error for a smaller norm. On the training points $(0,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ (noise on a vanishing labeling function), the minimum training error is $0$, attained by the interpolant; yet for large $\lambda$, $w_\lambda$ is close to $0$ with training error close to $1$ — not a least-squares solution. Only in the limit $\lambda\to0$ does ridge return a member of $W$. That the ridge minimizer is *unique* (the $m\lambda I$ term) says it breaks ties among *its own* objective's solutions, not among ERM's.

**4.5 — True.** Non-uniqueness means that some nonzero direction $v$ leaves every training prediction unchanged ($w$ and $w+tv$ have identical predictions on $S$ for all $t$ — that is precisely what multiple minimizers are, since the training error cannot tell them apart). A new training point must leave *its* prediction unchanged too, so the set of such directions shrinks as data accrues — ambiguity can only be eliminated, never created; and note that the labels play no role in the argument, so uniqueness is a property of the observed *inputs* alone. Restoring uniqueness: take the single training point $(0,5)$ (sensor reading $0$, label $5$), for which every $w$ with $w_0=5$ minimizes, and append a second training point $(1,5)$ — sensor reading $1$, same label. The first point forces $w_0=5$, the second forces $w_0+w_1=5$, and together they pin down $w_1=0$: the affine hypothesis is now fixed on both observed inputs, a unique solution.
